# Game shapes

Use a game shape to expose the important interaction, not to decorate the answer with terminology. Several shapes may coexist. Name only those that change the recommendation.

## Coordination

Actors benefit from choosing compatible actions but may disagree about which compatible outcome wins.

Look for:

- multiple stable outcomes;
- standards, conventions, focal points, and switching costs;
- assurance that others will move too;
- a credible first cohort or visible commitment.

Useful interventions include a shared deadline, default, pilot group, compatibility layer, or public progress signal. The central question is often how to move expectations, not how to defeat another actor.

## Bargaining

Actors can create or divide value but disagree over terms.

Map each side's outside option, cost of delay, information, authority, and ability to commit. A stated walk-away point is not necessarily the real one. Sequence concessions so they buy information or reciprocity rather than simply reveal urgency.

Use BATNA and ZOPA only when the alternatives and feasible agreement range have concrete support. When the range is unknown, identify the evidence needed to estimate it.

## Signaling and screening

One side has private information about type, quality, cost, or intent.

A signal is informative only when different types face meaningfully different costs or constraints. Cheap statements that every type would make carry little information. Screening changes the choices offered so types reveal themselves through behavior.

Check whether the signal is observable, costly enough to separate types, and aligned with later action.

## Commitment

An actor wants to change another actor's choice by constraining its own future behavior.

Commitments can come from contracts, deposits, deadlines, public promises, sunk investments, delegated authority, or technical architecture. Test credibility backward: when the future decision arrives, will following through still be in the actor's interest or control?

Preserve an escape hatch when uncertainty makes full commitment reckless.

## Principal-agent

The person delegating work and the person performing it have different information or incentives.

Inspect measurement, reward timing, hidden action, selection effects, and who bears downside. Avoid treating better monitoring as the only remedy; improve goal alignment, decision rights, feedback, and consequences for distorted metrics.

Include intermediaries when their incentives shape what reaches the principal.

## Repeated interaction

Today's move changes reputation, expectations, and future cooperation.

Ask whether actors can identify one another, observe behavior, attribute outcomes, and respond later. A repeated-game strategy needs a credible response to both cooperation and defection. Excessive punishment can destroy future surplus or invite escalation.

Distinguish a one-time exception from a precedent others can invoke.

## Entry, deterrence, and competition

An incumbent and entrant choose whether to enter, accommodate, invest, price, bundle, or retreat.

Test threats by backward induction. If fighting entry is costly after entry occurs, an unsupported threat may not deter. Capacity, contracts, product integration, customer switching costs, and regulation can change the later incentives and make a commitment credible.

Do not assume competitors optimize one metric or possess complete information.

## Escalation and attrition

Actors incur ongoing costs while waiting for the other side to yield, or risk increasingly destructive responses.

Map cost asymmetry, private resolve, misperception, face-saving exits, communication channels, and accidental escalation. A move that looks weak in a one-shot payoff table may be valuable if it creates a safe off-ramp or verifies intent.

Set stop conditions before entering an escalation path.

## Collective action and coalitions

Many actors prefer a shared outcome but each may prefer others to pay for it.

Inspect contribution visibility, free riding, threshold effects, coalition stability, and distribution of benefits. A minimum viable coalition, selective incentive, matching commitment, or contribution rule may matter more than universal agreement.

## Sequential decisions and option games

Later choices depend on what early moves reveal or foreclose.

Draw the sequence when timing matters. Compare commit-now value with the value of waiting, staging, experimenting, or buying an option. Waiting is not free when rivals learn, deadlines expire, or capabilities decay.
